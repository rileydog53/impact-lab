# Version 0.7 — Evolving material damage and fracture

Open **Assembly → New / Examples → Load bending fracture example** and run for 115 ms. The downward striker bends an eligible specimen over two freely moving supports. A separate receiver below the span can contact the detached pieces. Build the same experiment from a blank assembly with ordinary blocks and enable **Material fracture** on the specimen. No mechanical link or assigned crack path is required. Move the striker from −18 mm to +23 mm in X to change where the first through-crack forms.

The body inspector sets tensile/shear strength as a percentage of Young’s modulus and the cohesive fracture energy in J/m². Select a result field to inspect Cauchy von Mises stress in kPa, accumulated equivalent plastic strain in percent, interface damage from 0 to 1, or the distance between matching points on damaged faces in mm. Select the specimen and expand its fracture readout for piece mass, center, velocity, and the first contact with each newly exposed face. The associated chart can track crack length, separation, material fracture dissipation, or a sampled spatial stress peak. **Refine** pins the current trace and starts the same assembly with one finer mesh. **Compare** matches samples in simulated time. Saved assemblies retain material and resolution settings and rerun from their initial state.

## Numerical model and envelope

Each interior edge in an eligible mesh receives a zero-thickness cohesive interface integrated at two Gauss points. Bulk triangles keep their original constitutive law and lumped masses. The interface uses a bilinear law in effective separation, the norm of positive normal opening and tangential slip. Peak traction is the specified strength, and the area under the loading curve is the specified fracture energy. Initial interface stiffness scales with modulus divided by adjacent triangle altitude. The law is irreversible with secant unloading and independent, reversible compressive closure. The force includes the derivative of the rotating material frame to conserve net torque. Full failure requires both integration points to exhaust their cohesive traction.

The solver forms connected sets of triangles through the remaining interfaces at each break. Exposed faces become contact boundaries. A contact vertex averages the still-connected copies of a material node, and the transpose of that same map distributes its force. Detached pieces use the existing frictionless node–segment penalty contact with other pieces and assembly bodies. Matching-face compression hands off to ordinary sibling contact when the shared failed faces clear. Spring and breakable link anchors stay on their original material triangles.

This is a 2D plane-strain, mesh-edge model. Crack direction follows the available mesh edges. Compressive reclosure inside one connected piece uses the matching interface points. General self-contact of a folded piece and edge-only crossings are outside the contact model. The cohesive law uses the same strength and fracture energy for opening and sliding. The interfaces introduce finite pre-damage compliance. Forces, masses, and energies integrated over the section are per metre of out-of-plane depth. Use this for comparative numerical experiments. Predictive high-rate material failure, fragment statistics, and converged local crack-tip stress require further modeling and validation. Fracture runs stop on element or interface collapse, excessive contact overlap, or an energy imbalance over 2%.

## Verification

Run `swift run --disable-sandbox -c release ImpactCheck --fracture` for the focused checks, or omit the flag for the preset and assembly regressions too. The cohesive patch checks mixed-mode work against fracture energy times edge length, unloading, force gradients, and net force and torque. The assembled impact checks mass during each topology change, linear and angular momentum, the energy budget, receiver contact on an exposed face, and sensitivity to load location. The suite also checks J₂ plasticity and material damage coexisting in one specimen, estimates intact compliance with a weak pulse, and compares timesteps and meshes independently.

In the standard example the first split was at 11.931 ms near X = −18.11 mm. The receiver hit a newly exposed face at 65.048 ms, and its final center speed was 0.224 m/s. Moving the striker 41 mm moved the first split to X = +27.17 mm. Halving the standard timestep changed the first split time by 0.08% and preserved the four final pieces. Peak standard energy imbalance was 0.034%. The mixed-mode patch absorbed 0.13999981 J/m of integrated work against 0.14000000 J/m predicted. The weak pulse arrived 0.91% later with the cohesive mesh and produced no damage.

At the same 1.062 µs step, fine and extra-fine meshes shifted the first split time by 7.47% and the sampled peak stress by 7.13%. Sampled stress increased from 46.29 to 76.51 to 81.97 kPa across the three resolutions. Final crack lengths were 78.00, 86.20, and 57.16 mm. Neither finer mesh reproduced the new-face receiver contact at the common step. These observables have not converged. The 115 ms extra-fine run used 3,348 triangles and completed in 13.45 s headless on the M4 MacBook Air, in a verification process with 17.50 MiB peak resident memory.
